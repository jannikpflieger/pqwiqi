## What is Decoherence

To define decoherence, it is easier to start with the opposite and explain what coherence is. Many things are unique to quantum mechanics, such as [entanglement](/en/posts/entries/college/entanglement) or [superposition](/en/posts/entries/college/superposition), and as long as a quantum system stays coherent, it is described by these characteristics.

We can be precise about what "coherent" means. A qubit in superposition is written

$$
|\psi\rangle = a|0\rangle + b|1\rangle
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are complex amplitudes, each carrying a magnitude and a **phase**. The probabilities of measuring 0 or 1 are $|a|^2$ and $|b|^2$, which discard the phases entirely — so if outcomes were all we cared about, phase would be irrelevant. But phase is precisely what makes quantum computing work: [interference](/en/posts/entries/college/interference) between computational paths depends on relative phase, and algorithms are built by arranging for wrong answers to cancel and right ones to reinforce. **Coherence** means that relative phase is still well defined.

Decoherence is the loss of it to the environment. Afterwards the system has the same measurement statistics but behaves like a classical weighted coin. The bigger the system, the faster this happens, which is why you don't notice quantum properties in everyday life [1].

## How does Decoherence happen

Decoherence is induced very easily and takes great effort to prevent, which is why quantum computers are built in special labs where environmental factors are kept to a minimum — even a bus on the road outside can disturb an experiment. Other factors are a slight rise in temperature, or other atoms in the system.

The picture of the environment "shaking" the qubit is intuitive but incomplete; the deeper mechanism is entanglement. When a qubit interacts with anything it is not isolated from, it becomes correlated with it:

$$
\left( a|0\rangle + b|1\rangle \right) \otimes |E_0\rangle
\;\longrightarrow\;
a|0\rangle|E_0\rangle + b|1\rangle|E_1\rangle
$$

The right-hand side is a perfectly coherent state of the *combined* system; nothing has been destroyed. But the environment now holds a record of which state the qubit is in, and if $|E_0\rangle$ and $|E_1\rangle$ are distinguishable it has effectively measured the qubit, so interference between the branches is no longer observable to us. This is why decoherence is called a "quantum leak": the information is not annihilated, it is dispersed into degrees of freedom nobody can track.

## Open systems and quantum channels

The formal version of the above is worth doing carefully, because it shows that decoherence requires no extra postulate — it is ordinary unitary evolution, seen from the inside.

Take a system $S$ and environment $E$, initially uncorrelated, with $E$ in a pure state $|e_0\rangle$. The joint evolution is unitary, and the system's state alone is what remains after tracing out the environment:

$$
\rho' = \mathcal{E}(\rho) = \mathrm{Tr}_E \left[ U \left( \rho \otimes |e_0\rangle\langle e_0| \right) U^\dagger \right]
$$

Inserting a complete basis $\{|e_k\rangle\}$ for the environment and defining the **Kraus operators**

$$
E_k \equiv \langle e_k | U | e_0 \rangle
$$

which act on the system alone, gives the **operator-sum representation** [2], [6]:

$$
\mathcal{E}(\rho) = \sum_k E_k \, \rho \, E_k^\dagger,
\qquad
\sum_k E_k^\dagger E_k = I
$$

The second condition enforces that probabilities still sum to one. Any map of this form is a **quantum channel**, and conversely every physically valid channel can be written this way. So unitary evolution of $S \cup E$, restricted to $S$, *is* a quantum channel — nothing more is needed to get irreversible dynamics out of reversible physics.

## The canonical channels

Two channels account for most of what happens to a real qubit.

**Amplitude damping** describes energy loss to a cold environment, with $\gamma$ the probability of decay:

$$
E_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \sqrt{1-\gamma} \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
E_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sqrt{\gamma} \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

$E_1$ implements the jump $|1\rangle \to |0\rangle$. Note that $E_0$ is *not* proportional to the identity: even the "no jump" outcome changes the state, because failing to detect an emitted photon is itself evidence. This channel is what $T_1$ measures.

**Phase damping** destroys coherence with no energy exchange, with parameter $p$:

$$
E_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \sqrt{1-p} \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
E_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \sqrt{p} \end{pmatrix}
$$

Applying these to a general $\rho$ leaves the diagonal untouched and multiplies the off-diagonals by $\sqrt{1-p}$. This is the uniquely quantum noise process, with no classical counterpart; the Math Note at the end derives it from scratch.

Written out, a single-qubit state is

$$
\rho =
\begin{pmatrix}
|a|^2 & a b^* \\
a^* b & |b|^2
\end{pmatrix}
$$

The diagonal entries are the measurement probabilities and the off-diagonals are the **coherences**, which phase damping drives exponentially to zero:

$$
\rho(t) =
\begin{pmatrix}
|a|^2 & a b^* e^{-t/T_2} \\
a^* b\, e^{-t/T_2} & |b|^2
\end{pmatrix}
$$

The end state is diagonal — a classical probability distribution over 0 and 1.

## Two clocks: $T_1$ and $T_2$

**$T_1$** is energy relaxation: the qubit decays from $|1\rangle$ towards $|0\rangle$, changing the populations. **$T_2$** is dephasing: the relative phase randomises with no energy exchanged, typically because a drifting field shifts the qubit frequency so it accumulates phase at the wrong rate. Relaxation also destroys phase, so it feeds into $T_2$, but not the reverse:

$$
\frac{1}{T_2} = \frac{1}{2 T_1} + \frac{1}{T_\phi}
$$

where $T_\phi$ is pure dephasing alone. Since $T_\phi > 0$, this bounds $T_2 \leq 2 T_1$. Approaching that bound means pure dephasing has been eliminated and the qubit is relaxation-limited, which is where the best devices now sit.

## Quantum Noise

Quantum noise is one of the main reasons decoherence happens. A famous inequality states that position and momentum cannot both be measured with unlimited accuracy, producing genuine fluctuations — shot noise in photon counting, projection noise in qubit readout — that no experimental setup can remove.

It is worth separating this from decoherence. The uncertainty principle produces randomness that is *irreducible*. Decoherence comes from uncontrolled coupling to an environment and *can* be reduced, by better isolation, better materials and error correction. If the two were the same thing, quantum computing would be hopeless; they are not.

In current hardware the dominant limitation is **two-level system defects** in amorphous oxides at surfaces and interfaces, which resonantly absorb energy at the qubit frequency. Progress against them has come from materials work rather than circuit redesign: tantalum on high-resistivity silicon has produced transmons with $T_1$ up to 1.68 ms and Hahn-echo $T_2$ exceeding $T_1$ [5].

## The NISQ era and what comes next

Today we are in the NISQ era — Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum, a term coined by John Preskill [3]. You can find out more in [a different article](/en/posts/entries/college/nisq).

The way out is quantum error correction, and the reason it works at all is subtle. Noise is *continuous* — amplitude damping has a continuous parameter $\gamma$ — while codes correct only a discrete set of errors. The resolution is that any single-qubit Kraus operator expands in the Pauli basis,

$$
E_k = \alpha_k I + \beta_k X + \gamma_k Y + \delta_k Z
$$

so measuring the code's stabilizers projects the corrupted state onto a single discrete Pauli error. The syndrome measurement **digitizes the noise for free**, and a code correcting $\{I, X, Y, Z\}$ on each qubit therefore corrects every single-qubit error, including small continuous rotations.

This only helps below a critical **threshold** error rate; above it, adding qubits adds more errors than it fixes. That threshold has now been crossed: Google's Willow processor ran surface-code memories where increasing the code distance by 2 suppressed the logical error rate by $\Lambda = 2.14 \pm 0.02$, reaching $0.143\%$ error per cycle on a 101-qubit distance-7 code, with the encoded qubit outliving the best physical qubit by $2.4 \pm 0.3$ [4]. The same work found rare correlated error events roughly once an hour, attributed to high-energy impacts across the chip — and correlated errors break the independence assumption behind the threshold theorem, so they cannot be fixed by enlarging the code.

## Math Note: Deriving Phase Damping from Random Phase Kicks

Phase damping can be derived from a single physical assumption: that the qubit accumulates a phase we cannot predict [2].

Suppose the qubit undergoes a rotation about the $z$-axis,

$$
R_z(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\theta} \end{pmatrix}
$$

where the angle $\theta$ is random — set, say, by a stray field of unknown strength. Acting on $|\psi\rangle = a|0\rangle + b|1\rangle$ gives $a|0\rangle + b e^{i\theta}|1\rangle$, so the density matrix for one particular kick is

$$
R_z(\theta)|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|R_z^\dagger(\theta) =
\begin{pmatrix} |a|^2 & a b^* e^{-i\theta} \\ a^* b\, e^{i\theta} & |b|^2 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Nothing has decayed yet — this is still a pure state, just rotated. The decay appears only when we average over our ignorance of $\theta$. Take $\theta$ to be Gaussian with mean zero and variance $2\lambda$:

$$
\rho = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\lambda}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
R_z(\theta)|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|R_z^\dagger(\theta)\, e^{-\theta^2/4\lambda} \, d\theta
$$

Only the off-diagonal entries depend on $\theta$, so the whole calculation reduces to one integral — the characteristic function of a Gaussian:

$$
\langle e^{i\theta} \rangle
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\lambda}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{i\theta} e^{-\theta^2/4\lambda} \, d\theta
= e^{-\lambda}
$$

Substituting back,

$$
\rho =
\begin{pmatrix} |a|^2 & a b^* e^{-\lambda} \\ a^* b\, e^{-\lambda} & |b|^2 \end{pmatrix}
$$

The populations are untouched and the coherences are suppressed by $e^{-\lambda}$. If the variance grows linearly in time, $\lambda = t/T_2$, this is exactly the exponential decay quoted earlier. Matching to the Kraus form of the previous section requires $\sqrt{1-p} = e^{-\lambda}$, so the two descriptions are the same channel written with different parameters.

Two things are worth drawing out. First, no individual run of the experiment loses anything — each phase kick is unitary and reversible. Decoherence appears only in the *average* over many runs, which is precisely why it is a loss of information rather than a loss of energy. Second, this is the formal version of the "quantum leak" from the opening section: what leaked is our knowledge of $\theta$, and if we could measure the environment well enough to learn it, we could undo the whole thing.
