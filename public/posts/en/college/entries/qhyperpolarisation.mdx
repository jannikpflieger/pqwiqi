# Quantum Hyperpolarisation

When normal scans, such as NMR or MRI, are used at room temperature, nuclei that behave like tiny spinning magnets will point in random directions, causing scans to detect roughly 1 out of every 100,000 spins. This is where Quantum Hyperpolarization comes into play. This forces the nucleus's spin to align in the desired direction, amplifying a weak, otherwise undetectable signal.

## The Problem: Boltzmann Doesn't Care About Your Experiment

A spin-1/2 nucleus like $ ^ 1 H $ or $ ^{13} C $ sitting in a magnetic field \(B_0\). The field splits its two spin states by an energy gap

$$\\Delta E = \gamma \hbar B_0,\$$

where $\gamma\$ is the nucleus's gyromagnetic ratio. At thermal equilibrium, the population of each state follows the Boltzmann distribution, giving a net polarization

$$P = \tanh\\left(\frac{\gamma \hbar B_0}{2k_BT}\right) \approx \frac{\gamma \hbar B_0}{2k_B T}.\$$

Plug in a clinical field of 3 T and body temperature, and you get $P \sim 10^{-6}\$. That's the whole problem in one number; essentially, all of the spins point randomly, and only a tiny sliver of excess aligns with the field. Since detectable signal is proportional to that excess, thermal NMR is inherently signal-starved because of basic statistical mechanics.

## The Trick: Steal Order From Other Source

Hyperpolarization prevents the nuclear spins from equilibrating with their local thermal bath. Instead, it pipes in order from an external source that's much easier to polarize, and dumps that order onto the nuclei before it has a chance to leak away. Three approaches dominate, and one of them is Spin-exchange optical pumping (SEOP). A circularly polarized laser optically pumps the electron spins of an alkali vapor (usually rubidium) into a highly polarized state. Collisions between the alkali atoms and a noble gas like ¹²⁹Xe or ³He transfer that polarization to the noble gas nuclei via the Fermi-contact hyperfine interaction. This routinely reaches 20–90% polarization and is the standard route to hyperpolarized xenon gas used in lung MRI.

## Case in which Quantum Hyperpolarisation is used

Normal MRI machines work by detecting signals from water molecules in the body, which is why MRI is fantastic for imaging soft tissue like the brain or muscles. But lungs are mostly empty air space, with very little water, so on a regular MRI, lungs look like a dark, useless blur.

Hence, Cases such as early lung disease diagnosis are challenging to assess with proton-based lung MRI since they're mostly air with few water protons. Therefore, techniques such as hyperpolarized gas MRI use the spin-1/2 isotope $^3He$. 

>When human hyperpolarized gas MRI studies are carried out, the subject typically inhales the gas from a small plastic bag while positioned inside the bore, and the measurement starts immediately at breath-hold. Helium remains almost entirely within the air spaces after inhalation and does not cause any serious side effects after a single breath. _Source:Kern AL, Vogel-Claussen J. Scientists are also using the hyperpolarization technique in quantum sensing. Physicists used a single defect in a diamond crystal called a nitrogen-vacancy (NV) center as an incredibly precise quantum probe to hyperpolarize nearby molecules, achieving this at room temperature without requiring extreme cold or bulky equipment. This makes the adaptation of hyperpolarisation favorable because it is cheaper.

In conclusion, hyperpolarization turns a statistical-mechanics limitation into an engineering problem, making it solvable. Instead of accepting the tiny equilibrium polarization set by $k_BT$, using circularly polarized light with ordered photons to steal order and race to use it before it decays. The same structure drives an MRI scanner imaging a patient's lungs and a diamond crystal defect hyperpolarising a nuclear spin qubit, demonstrating the widespread application of hyperpolarisation.
