## What is spin?

In physics, we study the rotation of rigid bodies, such as spinning tops, which possess *angular momentum*. This motion can be broken down into orbital motion and spinning motion — an object is spinning if it rotates about its own central axis.

![Patch](images/angular_momentum.png)

The angular momentum $\mathbf{L}$ of a rigid body encodes its resistance to changes in rotation. It tells us how much torque $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ (rotational force) must be applied to change its state of rotation:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\boldsymbol{\tau}
$$

For instance, a spinning top keeps spinning even though gravity weighs it down to one side.

![Patch](images/gravity.png)

The component of angular momentum associated with spinning motion is what we call *spin*, $\mathbf{S}$, and it is proportional to the angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega}$:

$$
\mathbf{S}=I\boldsymbol{\omega}
$$

where we call $I$ the moment of inertia.

## What does spin have to do with quantum physics?

So far, spin looks like a classical concept, so why does it appear all the time in quantum physics?

Imagine an electron orbiting a proton. This is the classical picture of a spinning hydrogen atom:

![Patch](images/classical_atom.png)

This toy model produces a magnetic moment $\boldsymbol{\mu}\propto\mathbf{S}$ (the atom is similar to a tiny bar magnet!) and resists changes in orientation, just like real atoms.

At extremely small scales, however, spin behaves unexpectedly for two main reasons:

### 1. Spin is quantised

In 1922, Walther Gerlach and Otto Stern showed that spin takes discrete values.

They shone a beam of silver atoms through an inhomogeneous magnetic field. The interaction between an atom and the imposed magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ has an energy

$$
E=-\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{B}
$$

By varying the external field along the axis of the experiment, they could produce a force

$$
\mathbf{F}=\nabla(\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{B})
$$

which split the trajectories of Ag atoms depending only on $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ (and ultimately, spin).

![Patch](images/stern-gerlach.png)

The result? Silver atoms had only two possible spin orientations (up/down), as opposed to any possible random orientation. In fact, their spin takes on the precise values $\pm\hbar/2$, with $\hbar\approx 1.05 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$ being the reduced Planck constant.

In modern-day quantum mechanics, we describe observable quantities such as spin with operators (matrices). Their matrix eigenvalues correspond to the possible outcomes of an experiment, e.g., $\pm\hbar/2$. Explicitly, the quantum spin-$\hbar/2$ vector has components

$$
S_x=\frac{\hbar}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
S_y=\frac{\hbar}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\ i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
S_z=\frac{\hbar}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}
$$

These operators act on a two-dimensional complex vector space termed a Hilbert space, representing the possible spin states (up/down). The total spin operator squared yields a scalar multiple of the identity matrix, $\mathbf{I}$:

$$
S^2 = S_x^2 + S_y^2 + S_z^2 = \frac{3\hbar^2}{4}\mathbf{I}
$$

Moreover, the components of spin satisfy the following fundamental commutation relation:

$$
[S_x, S_y] = S_xS_y - S_yS_x = i\hbar S_z
$$

and similar relations hold with coordinates rotated cyclically: $(x\rightarrow y\rightarrow z\rightarrow x)$. Additionally, the components $S_x, S_y, S_z$ act like generators of rotations for the quantum system along the x, y, and z axes!

### 2. Fundamental particles have spin

In classical physics, particles are just points in space and therefore *cannot* spin by themselves. However, experiments motivated by Stern and Gerlach have been done on electrons — they have spin and it is quantised!

The classical picture breaks down even further: if spin were literal rotation and electrons were actually charged spheres, they would need to spin faster than light in order to match known experimental data! 

Consider the "classical electron radius" $r=2.8\times 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}$, electron mass $m=9.1\times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$, and the moment of inertia $I=\frac{2}{5}mr^2$ of a solid sphere. If the electron's spin magnitude is $\hbar/2$, this allows us to deduce that its angular frequency is

$$
\omega=\frac{5\hbar}{4mr^2}
$$

This means that the equator would be rotating at speeds of

$$
v=r\omega=\frac{5\hbar}{4mr}\approx 170c
$$

Why do we still talk about spin then? Just like a rotating charged object, the electron has a magnetic field *which resists changes in orientation*. However, it is not physically spinning — its angular momentum is another fundamental property, like mass or charge.

A deeper mathematical insight is that the spin quantum number $s$ only comes in integers and half-integers, i.e., $s=0, \frac{1}{2}, 1, \frac{3}{2}, 2, \dots$ We call half-integer spin particles, such as the electron, *fermions* — fundamental fermions make up matter. We call integer spin particles, such as the photon, *bosons* — fundamental forces are mediated by bosons. A surprising consequence of spin is the Pauli Exclusion Principle: two fermions cannot simultaneously occupy the same quantum state, whereas bosons can freely overlap.
