## Why do we need quantum cryptography?

Modern secure communication relies heavily on cryptographic keys.

In conventional public-key cryptography, the security of many protocols is based on computational assumptions: certain mathematical problems are believed to be extremely difficult for classical computers to solve.

Quantum computation changes this picture.

Algorithms such as **Shor's algorithm** can efficiently solve integer factorization and discrete logarithm problems on a sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer, threatening several widely used cryptographic schemes.

## A different idea: Quantum Key Distribution

Quantum Key Distribution, or **QKD**, follows a different philosophy.

Instead of basing security exclusively on computational complexity, QKD exploits fundamental properties of quantum mechanics to allow two parties to detect information leakage while establishing a secret key.

One of the fundamental QKD protocols is **BB84**, proposed by Bennett and Brassard in 1984.

## Encoding the key

Suppose Alice wants to establish a secret binary key with Bob.

She randomly generates two classical bit strings:

* one determines the values of the key bits;
* the other determines the basis used to encode each bit.

For every bit, Alice prepares a photon in one of four possible quantum states belonging to two different bases.

![Pic](/posts/assets/entries/qkd/basis.png)
*Principles of BB84 protocol [6]*

## Two quantum bases

Using polarization as an example, we can define the computational basis

$$
|0\rangle, \qquad |1\rangle
$$

and the diagonal basis

$$
|+\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle + |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},
\qquad
|-\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle - |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}.
$$

Alice can therefore encode a classical 0 or 1 either in the $Z$ basis,

$$
{|0\rangle, |1\rangle},
$$

or in the $X$ basis,

$$
{|+\rangle, |-\rangle}.
$$

She randomly chooses the basis for every photon and sends the resulting states to Bob.

## Bob performs the measurements

Bob does not know which basis Alice used.

For each incoming photon, he therefore randomly chooses either the $Z$ or the $X$ basis and performs a measurement.

If Bob chooses the same basis as Alice, he ideally reconstructs Alice's bit with certainty.

If he chooses the wrong basis, however, the result is random.

For example,

$$
|\langle + | 0 \rangle|^2
=
|\langle - | 0 \rangle|^2
=
\frac{1}{2}
$$

## Sifting the key

After the quantum transmission, Alice and Bob communicate through an **authenticated classical channel**.

They publicly reveal which bases they used, but they do not reveal their bit values.

Whenever they used different bases, the corresponding result is discarded.

They retain only the cases in which their bases matched.

This procedure is known as **sifting**.

![Pic](/posts/assets/entries/qkd/BB84.png)
*BB84 scheme [3]*

## Eve intercepts the photons

Now consider an eavesdropper, Eve.

The simplest possible strategy is the **intercept-and-resend attack**.

Eve intercepts each photon, randomly chooses a basis, measures the state and prepares a new photon corresponding to her result before sending it to Bob.

The problem is that Eve does not know Alice's preparation basis.

## Measurement causes disturbance

Eve chooses the wrong basis with probability

$$
P_{\text{wrong basis}} = \frac{1}{2}.
$$

When she measures in the wrong basis, the original state is projected onto one of the states of her chosen basis.

The information about Alice's original preparation is therefore partially destroyed.

If Bob later measures in Alice's correct basis, he can obtain the wrong result.

![Pic](/posts/assets/entries/qkd/eve.png)
*Eve's interception [5]*

## The 25% error rate

Suppose Alice and Bob keep only the events in which they used the same basis.

Eve chooses the wrong basis with probability

$$
\frac{1}{2},
$$

and in those cases Bob obtains the wrong bit with probability

$$
\frac{1}{2}.
$$

Therefore, the total probability of an error caused by an ideal intercept-and-resend attack is

$$
P_{\mathrm{error}}
=
\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2}
=
\frac{1}{4}
$$

This corresponds to an ideal quantum bit error rate of

$$
\mathrm{QBER} = 25%.
$$

The disturbance introduced by Eve can therefore be detected statistically.

## Why can't Eve copy the state?

Eve might try another strategy: instead of measuring the photon, why not simply copy it?

Quantum mechanics prevents this through the **no-cloning theorem**.

There is no universal quantum operation capable of perfectly copying an arbitrary unknown quantum state:

$$
|\psi\rangle |0\rangle
\not\longrightarrow
|\psi\rangle |\psi\rangle
$$

for every possible $|\psi\rangle$.

Eve therefore cannot simply keep one perfect copy of the state and send another undisturbed copy to Bob.

## Estimating the error rate

After sifting, Alice and Bob still do not immediately possess a secure final key.

Real experiments contain losses, imperfect detectors and environmental noise. Errors can therefore appear even when Eve is absent.

Alice and Bob publicly compare a randomly selected fraction of their sifted bits and estimate the **quantum bit error rate**

$$
Q =
\frac{N_{\mathrm{errors}}}{N_{\mathrm{compared}}}.
$$

If the QBER exceeds the security threshold of the protocol, they abort the communication.

## Error correction

If the error rate is sufficiently low, Alice and Bob continue with classical post-processing.

The first step is **information reconciliation**, or error correction.

This allows them to identify and correct differences between their bit strings until both parties possess identical keys.

## Privacy amplification

Error correction alone is not enough.

Eve may still have obtained partial information about the key without creating enough errors to force Alice and Bob to abort.

Alice and Bob therefore perform **privacy amplification**.

This procedure compresses the partially secure key into a shorter final key, reducing Eve's information to an arbitrarily small level.

## Secret-key rate

In an asymptotic security analysis, the obtainable secret-key rate can be expressed schematically as

$$
R \sim I(A:B) - \chi(E),
$$

where $I(A:B)$ represents the correlations shared by Alice and Bob, while $\chi(E)$ represents an upper bound on the information accessible to Eve.

The exact expression depends on the protocol, implementation and assumed noise model.

## Why authentication matters

The classical channel used by Alice and Bob does not need to be secret.

However, it **must be authenticated**.

Without authentication, Eve could perform a man-in-the-middle attack: she could establish one QKD connection with Alice while pretending to be Bob, and another with Bob while pretending to be Alice.

QKD therefore does not remove the need for authentication.

Instead, it can use a small initial authentication secret to establish much larger amounts of new secret key material.

## QKD does not encrypt the message

An important distinction is that QKD does not itself encrypt or transmit the secret message.

Its purpose is **key establishment**.

Once Alice and Bob share a secure secret key, they can use it with conventional symmetric encryption.

In principle, the key can even be combined with a **one-time pad**.

If the key is truly random, used only once and at least as long as the message, the resulting encryption provides information-theoretic security.

## Computational vs physical security

The central difference between conventional public-key cryptography and QKD lies in the origin of their security.

Classical public-key schemes usually rely on assumptions about what an adversary can compute.

QKD instead allows security to be proven from a physical model based on quantum mechanics, even if Eve is assumed to possess extremely powerful computational resources.

## Real devices are not ideal

This does not mean that every practical QKD system is automatically perfectly secure.

Real devices can contain imperfections such as detector side channels, imperfect photon sources, losses and calibration errors.

Modern QKD research therefore distinguishes carefully between the security of an ideal protocol and the security of its physical implementation.

## The central idea

Quantum Key Distribution turns one of the most unusual features of quantum mechanics into a technological resource: measuring an unknown quantum state is not always a passive act.

By monitoring disturbances in the quantum communication channel, Alice and Bob can estimate how much information an eavesdropper could have obtained.

They can then process their data to extract a final key whose secrecy is ultimately based on the laws of quantum physics.
