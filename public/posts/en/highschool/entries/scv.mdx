## What Is Superposition?

Superposition is one of the main features of quantum mechanics that differentiate it from classical mechanics. It is the phenomenon where some variable (like a particle) can be in more than one state at the same timeas explained in our [article about superposition](/posts/en/highschool/entries/superposition).
For example, when we put a cat in a box and we close it, the cat will be either dead or alive, depending on how much time it spends there. However, in the quantum world, since we cannot see it, we assume that it is both death and alive at the same time.

## Discrete and Continuous Variables

This concept, although difficult to assimilate, is clear when we talk about discrete states; that is, when the particle can be one or the other, but not some intermediate state. A discrete variable would be up and down, left or right. The opposite is a continuous value; for example, all the grey colours between black and white.

Superposition becomes intuitively more complex when talking about continuous variables than discrete variables. And the cannot be avoided, since a lot of physical quantities are continuous (position and velocity of a particle for starters). Let’s try to understand it by looking at the position of a particle.

## Where Is the Particle?

If we tried to see where our cat is when we leave the room, we can think that it can be in our bed or in our chair. But the cat can also be on the table, inside the wardrobe or anywhere in the floor. That means that the position is a continuous variable. 
In the classical world we know it is just in one place, but what do we do in the quantum world?

To think about that we will first think about one person, Mike. Mike is a regular guy, who really enjoys running. Every day he goes running and takes the same route. When thinking about where Mike can be 5 minutes after he left the house we consider how fast Mike runs and where he usually is after 5 minutes. But we never say a specific spot; we say an area. We have painted that area in blue in the map. That area is centred around a place where we think it’s most likely for him to be; so it is more likely that Mike is in the middle of the area, and less likely as we go further to the edge.

If we now try to think where Mike will be after 30 minutes, we do the same thing. But after 30 minutes many things can happen. Some days Mike is full of energy and runs very fast. Other days he finds his friends and stays talking for a while. So no, when we think about where Mike can be, we think of a broader area. That area is painted in yellow in the picture. Again, it is more likely for Mike to be in the middle of that area than at the beginning or the end.

## Describing Position with a Probability Function

The way physicists describe this information is with a probability function. In this particular case, it will be a normally distributed function; where the maximum probability is in the central position, and it is less likely as we go further from the position. Also, the smaller the region we think Mike will be in, the higher the function grows.

![From Mike to Probability function](/posts/assets/entries/scv/probfunc.png) 
_From Mike to Probability Functions of his Positions_

## From Mike to a Quantum Particle

These functions are also used to represent quantum particles. The difference between the quantum particle and Mike is that we know Mike will be in one place, but the particle is occupying all the region we have defined at the same time. Why use probability functions then? Because the particle has not equal “presence” in all of the region.

## What Happens When We Measure?

If we run a experiment on a quantum Mike, we will find Mike in every place of the regions; but we will find him more often at the centre. The particle is everywhere in the region until we measure. When we measure, we say that the probability function collapses, which means that then the particle is only at one place. This is represented by a delta-function; a normal function but that has an extremely highly defined point, so that in the graph it is really just a line. Then, with time, it will start spreading and occupying more space. The point where the line is corresponds to the position of the particle when we measure, and it is more often found in the point corresponding to the highest height of the probability function.
