# Quantum Simulation
In experimental physics, simulations are used to investigate the dynamics (or time evolution) of a physical system on a computer without preparing the investigated system in an experiment. This method provides insight into the system's dynamics while saving time and financial resources, since physical experiment preparation is not necessary. However, if the desired system is governed by quantum mechanics, the necessary calculations for the simulation are extremely complex and cannot be done in a reasonable amount of time on a classical computer, as they oftentimes scale exponentially with the size of the system, i.e., with the number of particles involved in the target system. Therefore, quantum systems are often not efficiently simulated on classical computers. 

## Systems that are simulatable via quantum mechanics
To simulate quantum systems, one has to employ so-called quantum simulations. For this, a physical system with known and controllable dynamics is engineered, which is called a quantum simulator, that mimics the dynamics of the investigated system. Since the quantum simulator itself is governed by quantum mechanics, this can happen in a reasonable amount of time (efficiently). 


## Choosing the correct platform depending on the dynamics of the system 

The field of quantum simulations uses platforms such as trapped ions or ultracold neutral atoms, which are also used in quantum computing. The important distinction is that quantum simulations are designed only to speed up calculations for a specific problem, whereas quantum computers aim to solve a variety of problems. 

The dynamics of a physical system are modeled by the time evolution of its wave function. The time evolution of the wave function can be derived as a solution of a certain differential equation, the so-called Schrödinger equation. To solve the Schrödinger equation, one needs the so-called Hamiltonian of the target system, which, in general, can be a very complex object, making the equation almost impossible to solve. 

Quantum simulation, therefore, comes down to simulating the dynamics generated by a given Hamiltonian in an efficient amount of time. For example, chemical reactions often need to be simulated. Since usually large amounts of atoms or molecules are involved in chemical reactions, carrying out calculations classically, e.g., for each atom separately, would take an enormous amount of time and therefore wouldn’t be feasible. The field of quantum simulations aims to engineer quantum simulators, which simulate such physical systems in an appropriate amount of time. 

In conclusion, quantum simulations are a crucial field of research as they possibly have a variety of important applications in science and industry.
