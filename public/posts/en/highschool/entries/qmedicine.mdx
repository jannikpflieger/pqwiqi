# Quantum Tech in Healthcare

When you hear the word **quantum**, you might picture science-fiction movies, futuristic machines, or complicated equations written on a chalkboard. But quantum physics is not just something that belongs in a physics textbook. The strange behavior of atoms and other tiny particles is already helping scientists develop new technologies for healthcare.

From more sensitive medical sensors to new approaches to drug discovery, quantum technology could change the way we understand and treat diseases.

So, how exactly can something happening at the scale of atoms help doctors and scientists understand the human body? Let's take a look.

## What Is "Quantum" Anyway?

Before we talk about quantum technology in healthcare, let's first understand what **quantum physics** actually means.

In our everyday lives, most things behave in ways that feel predictable. If you throw a ball, for example, you can calculate where it will go based on its speed, direction, and the forces acting on it. This is the world of **classical physics**.

But things get much stranger when we zoom in to the scale of atoms and subatomic particles. At this level, nature follows the rules of **quantum mechanics**, and these rules can be very different from what we experience in everyday life.

Take **superposition**, for example. A quantum system can exist in a combination of different possible states until it is measured.

Then there is **entanglement**. Two quantum systems can become strongly connected, meaning that measuring one can tell us something about the state of the other, even when they are far apart.

These ideas might sound like science fiction, but they have been demonstrated many times in experiments. Scientists are now learning how to control these quantum effects and use them to build useful technologies.

When these technologies are developed for healthcare and medicine, they are often referred to as **quantum medical technologies** or **quantum technologies in healthcare**.

## Three Ways Quantum Physics Could Change Medicine

### 1. Ultra-Precise Medical Imaging and Sensing

You might be surprised to learn that a technology you can already find in hospitals, the **MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging)** machine, relies on quantum physics.

![image.png](/posts/assets/entries/qmedicine/mri_machine.png)

_Image Source: [Riverwood Healthcare Center](https://riverwoodhealthcare.org/riverwood-healthcare-center-introduces-new-mri-machine/)*

An MRI scanner makes use of a property of atomic nuclei called **spin**. Hydrogen is especially useful because our bodies contain a lot of water, and water contains hydrogen.

Inside the powerful magnetic field of an MRI scanner, these hydrogen nuclei respond to radio waves. As they return to their original state, they produce signals that can be detected and turned into detailed images of the inside of the body.

But scientists are asking an interesting question: **Can we make sensors even more sensitive than the ones we use today?**

One possibility comes from something called **nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers in diamond**. These are tiny imperfections in a diamond's crystal structure. Surprisingly, these imperfections have useful quantum properties that scientists can control and measure.

NV centers can be extremely sensitive to magnetic fields. This makes them interesting for studying the tiny magnetic signals produced by biological activity.

* **Brain and heart activity:** Our brains and hearts produce electrical signals, which also create tiny magnetic fields. Very sensitive quantum sensors could help scientists measure these signals in greater detail.
* **Disease detection:** Scientists are also exploring whether these sensors could detect subtle changes in biological activity that might provide new ways of identifying diseases.

These applications are still being researched, so quantum sensors are not yet replacing conventional medical equipment. But their sensitivity makes them an exciting area of research.

### 2. Quantum Computing for Drug Discovery

Developing a new medicine is a long and complicated process. One important challenge is figuring out how a potential drug molecule interacts with a target in the body, such as a protein.

Scientists already use powerful computers to simulate these interactions. The problem is that molecules are quantum systems themselves. As molecules become more complicated, accurately describing how their electrons behave can become extremely difficult for classical computers.

This is one reason scientists are interested in **quantum computers**.

Instead of using ordinary bits that are either 0 or 1, quantum computers use **qubits**. Qubits can be manipulated using quantum effects such as superposition and entanglement.

Because molecules also follow the rules of quantum mechanics, quantum computers could eventually become useful for simulating certain molecular systems.

![image.png](/posts/assets/entries/qmedicine/quantum_comp.png)

_Image Source: [Jeremy Hsu, "Measuring Progress in the 'Noisy' Era of Quantum Computing"](https://spectrum.ieee.org/measuring-progress-in-the-noisy-era-of-quantum-computing)*

In the future, quantum computers could help scientists:

* simulate the electronic structure of molecules;
* study chemical reactions and molecular interactions;
* search for promising drug candidates;
* understand how potential medicines interact with proteins.

However, we are not there yet.

Today's quantum computers are still relatively small and can make errors because of **quantum noise and decoherence**. For now, classical computers remain much more useful for most drug-discovery tasks.

The hope is that as quantum computers become larger and more reliable, they could become a valuable addition to the tools scientists already use to develop new medicines.

### 3. Quantum Biology

Now for something even more fascinating: **quantum biology**.

Quantum biology asks a simple but surprisingly difficult question:

**Does nature make use of quantum mechanics inside living organisms?**

At first, this might seem unlikely. Quantum experiments are often performed in carefully controlled laboratories, while the human body is warm, wet, and incredibly complex.

Yet scientists have found evidence that quantum effects may play a role in some biological processes.

For example:

* **Enzymes:** Some enzymes may make use of **quantum tunneling**. In simple terms, this allows particles such as electrons or protons to pass through energy barriers that they would not be able to cross according to classical physics.
* **Photosynthesis:** Scientists have studied whether quantum effects help energy move efficiently through the molecular structures involved in photosynthesis.
* **DNA:** Researchers are also exploring whether quantum effects could influence some processes involved in DNA mutations. How important these effects are in real biological systems is still an open question.

![image.png](/posts/assets/entries/qmedicine/quantum_bio.png)

If we can understand how quantum effects are used by nature, we may discover new ways of understanding biological processes.

But there is an important distinction here. **Quantum biology does not mean that the human body is a giant quantum computer.** Instead, researchers are trying to find specific biological processes where quantum effects actually matter.

## The Future of Quantum Healthcare

So, are quantum technologies ready to completely change medicine?

Not yet. Many of these technologies are still being developed and tested. **Quantum sensing** is already being explored for medical applications, while quantum computing for drug discovery is still further away due to challenges such as noise and errors.

Still, the potential is exciting. Quantum sensors could help detect tiny signals from the human body, while quantum computers could eventually help scientists understand complex molecules and develop new medicines.

The goal is not to replace modern medicine with "quantum medicine," but to give doctors and researchers **new tools to see, measure, and understand the human body**.

More than a century after quantum physics began, its strange rules could help us tackle very human problems—from detecting diseases earlier to developing better treatments.
