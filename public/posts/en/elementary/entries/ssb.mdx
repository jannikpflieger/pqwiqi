Spontaneous symmetry breaking is a process by which a physical system reaches a state less “symmetrical” than the physical laws that describe it.

Think about a vertical one-dimensional elastic rod: if you rotate it on its axis, you won’t be able to distinguish the final state from the initial one: the rod has rotational symmetry. However, if you apply a vertical force, eventually the rod will bend. In which direction? Nobody knows!
Moreover, the system now is not rotationally invariant anymore. The force we applied respected the symmetry of the system, yet it just chose a random direction to bend: it spontaneously broke the symmetry!

(/posts/assets/entries/ssb/rod.png)
 
What happened with the rod happens in many other cases, leading to phase transitions: in the Ising model, for example, at 0 temperature the spins (the observable describing particles as tiny magnets) align. That can happen either all up or all down, thus breaking symmetry. Increasing the temperature reduces the magnetization, and at the so-called Curie temperature it reaches a symmetric state, meaning mixed up and down. That’s a phase transition, from ferromagnetic to paramagnetic phase!

![Example of an Ising model configuration](/posts/assets/entries/ssb/ising.png)


Great things can happen due to spontaneous symmetry breaking: a weakly interacting gas that condenses breaks higher symmetries as well and becomes a superfluid with a lot of exciting properties! But pay attention, they are fragile: even at absolute zero temperature quantum fluctuations can destroy this state.
