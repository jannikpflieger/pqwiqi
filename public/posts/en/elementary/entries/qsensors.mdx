How can we measure something extremely small, such as a tiny magnetic field or a slight change in gravity? Classical sensors usually detect a quantity directly, for example by measuring a voltage, a displacement, or an intensity. However, their precision is limited by noise and by how small a signal they can resolve.

Quantum sensors take a different approach. Instead of measuring a quantity directly, they use a quantum system - such as an atom, a photon, or an electron spin - as a probe. The system is first prepared in a well-defined state. It is then allowed to interact with the environment, where the quantity of interest slightly modifies its evolution. Finally, the system is measured, and this change reveals information about the quantity.

A key difference is that the information is often encoded in a phase that builds up over time. This allows quantum sensors to detect extremely small effects with high precision, often beyond classical limits.

Different physical platforms can be used as quantum sensors. Atomic systems, such as atomic clocks and atom interferometers, are widely used for precise measurements of time and gravity. Solid-state systems, including spins in diamond known as nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers, enable sensing at very small scales. Superconducting devices such as SQUIDs are among the most sensitive magnetometers, while optical systems using quantum light can further enhance measurement precision.
