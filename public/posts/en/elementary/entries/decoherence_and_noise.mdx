## What is Decoherence

To start with a definition of decoherence, it's actually easier to start with the opposite and explain what coherence is. As explained in other articles, there are many things that are unique to quantum mechanics, such as entanglement or superposition. As long as a quantum system is in coherence, it's described by these characteristics. Decoherence therefore means the loss of quantum information to its environment. The bigger a system is, the more likely decoherence appears, which is why you don't notice quantum properties in your everyday life.

## How does Decoherence happen

Unfortunately, decoherence is induced very easily and it takes great effort to prevent it. This is why current quantum computers are built in special labs, where scientists try to keep environmental factors to a minimum. Even the bus driving on the road next to the research building can influence an experiment and mess up your measurement. Other factors can be a slight rise in temperature (quantum computers are kept very cold, to be able to arrange precise movements of particles) or even other atoms in the quantum system. 

Often times, when decoherence appears, information is lost. This is why decoherence is sometimes also called "quantum leak" or "information leak", since there is no way for us to get this information back. This is currently a big problem when building quantum computers.

## Quantum Noise

Quantum noise is one of the main reasons why decoherence happens. It is also (unfortunately) one of the central points of quantum objects.

A famous equation states that it is impossible to both measure the location and momentum with precise accuracy. This uncertainty leads to fluctuations in measurement, which are defined as quantum noise. To our knowledge, there is no way of getting around this, even with a perfect experiment setup.

Today, we are in the NISQ era of quantum computers, with the N standing for noisy. You can find out more about this in a different article.
