## What Is Superposition?

Superposition is one of the main features of quantum mechanics that differentiate it from classical mechanics. It is the phenomenon where some variable (like a particle) can be in more than one state at the same time, as explained in our [article about superposition](/posts/en/elementary/entries/superposition).

For example, when we put a cat in a box and we close it, the cat will be either dead or alive, depending on how much time it spends there. However, in the quantum world, since we cannot see it, we assume that it is both dead and alive at the same time.

## Discrete and Continuous Variables

This concept, although difficult to assimilate, is clear when we talk about discrete states; that is, when the particle can be one or the other, but not some intermediate state.

A discrete variable would be up and down, left or right. The opposite is a continuous value; for example, all the grey colours between black and white.

## But Where Is the Particle?

One of the most basic things to know when we want to do an experiment is to know where the particle is (if not, we can’t do anything to the particle!). But now we have a problem: superposition.

If we tried to see where our cat is when we leave the room, we can think that it can be in our bed or in our chair. But the cat can also be on the table, inside the wardrobe or anywhere on the floor.

That means that position is a continuous variable. In the classical world we know it is just in one place, but what do we do in the quantum world?

To think about that, we will first think about one person, Mike.

Mike is a regular guy who really enjoys running. Every day he goes running and takes the same route. When thinking about where Mike can be 5 minutes after he left the house, we consider how fast Mike runs and where he usually is after 5 minutes.

But we never say a specific spot; we say an area. We have painted that area in blue on the map. That area is centred around a place where we think it is most likely for him to be, so it is more likely that Mike is in the middle of the area and less likely as we go further towards the edge.

If we now try to think where Mike will be after 30 minutes, we do the same thing. But after 30 minutes many things can happen.

Some days Mike is full of energy and runs very fast. Other days he finds his friends and stays talking for a while. So now, when we think about where Mike can be, we think of a broader area.

That area is painted in yellow in the picture. Again, it is more likely for Mike to be in the middle of that area than at the beginning or the end.

## From Mike to a Quantum Particle

If instead of thinking about Mike we were thinking about a particle, we would do something very similar.

What scientists do is consider an area and, based on the surroundings, determine where the particle is more likely to be. Based on that, they create a map that says in which places it is very likely for the particle to be, in which places it is almost impossible, and which places are intermediate.


When the map is ready, we say that the particle is everywhere on the map at the same time, but with different probabilities.

That way, the particle is everywhere, but when we measure it, we will find the particle more often in the high-probability areas.
